Uncertainty ​

An experimental value is only useful together with how well it is known. This page explains what the archive records, how ThermophysicalDB.jl restates it on a common basis, and how to use that basis to weight data from different laboratories.

The terms used here (standard and expanded uncertainty, coverage factor , level of confidence) follow the Guide to the expression of uncertainty in measurement (BIPM et al., undated); how ThermoML records them is described in (Chirico et al., 2003).

Standard and expanded uncertainty ​

A standard uncertainty is the estimated standard deviation of a measured value. Laboratories usually publish an expanded uncertainty,

where the coverage factor widens the interval so that it contains the true value with a stated probability, the level of confidence . Two publications can both say "uncertainty 0.5 %" and mean different things: one may report with   (about 95 %), another with   (about 68 %). Comparing, or weighting, them without bringing them to one basis would be an error of a factor of two.

What the archive stores ​

In the archive snapshot used by this package, every uncertainty is an expanded combined uncertainty accompanied by a level of confidence for the property (stored as a percentage, almost always 95 %). A coverage factor is not always stated. ThermophysicalDB.jl keeps exactly what was published, and adds one derived column:

ColumnContent
uncthe uncertainty as published, in the unit of value
unc_kthe coverage factor given by the source, or NaN
unc_levelthe level of confidence as a fraction (0.95, not 95), or NaN
unc_k2 restated at  , or NaN when that cannot be done without inventing data

Nothing is overwritten and the derived column is computed on demand, so changing the rule never leaves stale numbers on disk.

How unc_k2 is derived ​

For every point, in this order:

  1. If the source gave a coverage factor,   . No assumption is needed.

  2. Otherwise, if it gave a level of confidence, follows from the level through the policy (below), and   .

  3. Otherwise there is no basis to rescale, and the result is NaN.

A level of confidence does not determine by itself: that requires assuming a distribution. For a normal distribution, is the two-sided quantile ; by the convention of the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement, 95 % is expressed with the round value  . The two readings differ by about 2 %, so both are available through the keyword policy of table:

Only these levels are accepted. For any other level the package does not guess: unc_k2 is NaN for those points, and a single warning counts how many there were.

using ThermophysicalDB, CairoMakie, Unitful, Statistics

ThermophysicalDB.coverage_factor(0.95), ThermophysicalDB.coverage_factor(0.95; policy = :normal)
(2.0, 1.959963984540054)

Weighting data from many laboratories ​

Data from different sources can be combined by weighting each point with the inverse of its variance. Because unc_k2 puts every point on the same , weights proportional to are comparable between articles. As an example, take the density of pure water at 25 °C and 1 atm:

julia
db = database(progress = false)
v = points(
    db; component = "water", exact = true, property = "Mass density",
    temperature = (24.95u"°C", 25.05u"°C"), pressure = (0.98u"atm", 1.02u"atm"),
)
t = table(v; quantities = Unitful)

ρ = t.value
U = t.unc_k2
usable = isfinite.(U) .& (U .> zero(eltype(U)))

(points = length(ρ), with_uncertainty = count(usable))
(points = 273, with_uncertainty = 273)

The weighted mean follows from the weights, and so does its own uncertainty. For independent measurements with standard uncertainties   (since unc_k2 is at  ), the mean is   with  , and its standard uncertainty is  :

julia
u = U[usable] ./ 2                                # standard uncertainties
w = 1 ./ u .^ 2
ρ_mean = sum(w .* ρ[usable]) / sum(w)
U_mean = 2 / sqrt(sum(w))                         # expanded, k = 2

(ρ_mean = uconvert(u"kg/m^3", ρ_mean), U_mean = uconvert(u"kg/m^3", U_mean))
(ρ_mean = 997.0743211764207 kg m^-3, U_mean = 0.006293381024562723 kg m^-3)
julia
d = 1e6 .* Float64.((ρ[usable] .- ρ_mean) ./ ρ_mean)       # deviation in ppm
dU = 1e6 .* Float64.(U[usable] ./ ρ_mean)                    # stated U(k = 2) in ppm
order = sortperm(d)

fig = Figure(size = (760, 520))
ax = Axis(
    fig[1, 1]; xlabel = "deviation from the weighted mean [ppm]", ylabel = "points, sorted",
    title = "Water density at 25 °C: stated uncertainty U(k = 2)",
)
vlines!(ax, [0.0]; color = "#e8ecff", linewidth = 1.5)
errorbars!(ax, d[order], 1:length(order), dU[order]; direction = :x, color = (:gray, 0.55), whiskerwidth = 4)
scatter!(ax, d[order], 1:length(order); color = "#2fe0ff", markersize = 7)
fig

Since the bars are 95 % intervals, about one in twenty is expected to miss the zero line by chance. Many more than that means the stated uncertainties are optimistic.

Parity with a reference equation ​

The comparison above uses the data themselves as reference. Over a range of temperatures a reference equation plays that role, and a parity plot shows every point against it. Both axes share the same limits, so the parity line is the exact diagonal, and the dashed curves mark deviations of 1 %, 5 % and 10 %. The bars are the expanded uncertainties ( ): the stated of the experiment on , and that of the reference equation on . In the right panel each deviation is divided by the combined uncertainty : a point inside the band agrees with the equation within the uncertainties of both.

julia
using Clapeyron

vw = points(
    db; component = "water", exact = true, property = "Mass density",
    temperature = (0u"°C", 99u"°C"), pressure = (0.98u"atm", 1.02u"atm"),
)
tw = table(vw; quantities = Unitful)

Tw = tw.temperature
ρw = tw.value
Uw = tw.unc_k2

model = IAPWS95()
by_temperature = Dict(Ti => mass_density(model, 1u"atm", Ti) for Ti in unique(Tw))
ρref = [by_temperature[Ti] for Ti in Tw]

relative_U_ref = 1e-5                    # expanded relative uncertainty of IAPWS-95 in the density of liquid water
Uref = relative_U_ref .* ρref

has_U = isfinite.(Uw) .& (Uw .> zero(eltype(Uw)))
Utotal = sqrt.(Uw .^ 2 .+ Uref .^ 2)
z = Float64.((ρw .- ρref) ./ Utotal)

x = ustrip.(u"kg/m^3", ρref)
y = ustrip.(u"kg/m^3", ρw)

# the same limits on both axes: the parity line is the exact diagonal
lo, hi = 0.88 * minimum(x), 1.12 * maximum(x)
line = [lo, hi]

fig = Figure(size = (1000, 520))
ax1 = Axis(
    fig[1, 1]; xlabel = "ρ IAPWS-95 [kg m⁻³]", ylabel = "ρ experimental [kg m⁻³]",
    title = "Parity plot", aspect = DataAspect(),
)
limits!(ax1, lo, hi, lo, hi)
for (δ, style) in ((0.01, :dash), (0.05, :dashdot), (0.10, :dot))
    lines!(ax1, line, (1 + δ) .* line; color = (:gray, 0.8), linestyle = style, label = "±$(round(Int, 100δ)) %")
    lines!(ax1, line, (1 - δ) .* line; color = (:gray, 0.8), linestyle = style)
end
lines!(ax1, line, line; color = "#e8ecff", linewidth = 1.5, label = "parity")
errorbars!(ax1, x, y, ustrip.(u"kg/m^3", Uref); direction = :x, color = (:gray, 0.45), whiskerwidth = 4)
errorbars!(ax1, x[has_U], y[has_U], ustrip.(u"kg/m^3", Uw[has_U]); color = (:gray, 0.45), whiskerwidth = 4)
scatter!(ax1, x, y; color = ("#2fe0ff", 0.7), markersize = 7)
axislegend(ax1; position = :lt)

ax2 = Axis(
    fig[1, 2]; xlabel = "T [°C]", ylabel = "(ρ − ρ_IAPWS-95)/U",
    title = "Deviation in units of the combined uncertainty",
)
hlines!(ax2, [-1.0, 1.0]; color = (:gray, 0.8), linestyle = :dash)
hlines!(ax2, [0.0]; color = "#e8ecff", linewidth = 1.5)
scatter!(ax2, ustrip.(u"°C", Tw[has_U]), z[has_U]; color = ("#ffb000", 0.8), markersize = 7)
fig

The share of points inside the band is a direct measure of how realistic the stated uncertainties are:

julia
(with_uncertainty = count(has_U), inside_band = count(abs.(z[has_U]) .<= 1))
(with_uncertainty = 1354, inside_band = 1241)

Reading the two panels ​

The panels answer different questions, and neither contradicts the other.

  • Parity plot. Its axes span hundreds of kg m⁻³ so that the 10 % curves fit, while the data lie in a band of about 40 kg m⁻³ and differ from the equation by tens of ppm at most. A deviation of that size is far smaller than a marker, so almost every point sits on the diagonal. The plot shows that the data are correct to well within 1 %, and nothing finer than that.

  • Normalized deviation. Densimeters used for water usually state an uncertainty of a few ppm. Dividing a deviation of tens of ppm by such a small gives values of 5, 20 or more, so this panel judges each point against the precision its authors claim for it.

Of the points that state an uncertainty, about nine in ten fall inside . For intervals at the 95 % level about nineteen in twenty would be expected, so the stated uncertainties are realistic as a whole and slightly optimistic. The points far outside the band are concentrated near 25 °C, where most articles measured, and have usual suspects: a typing or unit error, water that was not degassed or whose isotopic composition differs from the reference (the equation describes VSMOW), and temperatures on an older scale than ITS-90, which alone shifts the density by tens of ppm. They are worth inspecting by DOI with points(vw; doi = "…").

The assumed uncertainty of the equation sets a floor to the combined . Where an experiment states less than , the width of the band is decided by relative_U_ref and not by the experiment.

Choosing the policy ​

Pick :nominal (the default) when you want the conventional reading of 95 % as  , which is what the original authors almost certainly meant. Pick :normal when you want the strictly normal-theory factor . The effect is a uniform 2 % scale on the affected uncertainties:

julia
nominal = table(v; policy = :nominal).unc_k2
normal = table(v; policy = :normal).unc_k2

extrema(filter(isfinite, normal ./ nominal))
(1.0204269138493078, 1.0204269138493078)

The output is the smallest and the largest ratio between the two versions of unc_k2, point by point. Both numbers are equal to  , and this is the expected result, not a coincidence of the two policies. A source that states an expanded uncertainty at the 95 % level has to be restated at  . With :nominal the 95 % level is taken as   already, so the value is kept. With :normal the coverage factor is 1.95996, and the value is multiplied by , which makes it 2 % larger. Equal minimum and maximum mean that every point with a finite uncertainty in this selection was stated at the 95 % level, so all of them change by the same factor. A selection that mixed levels would show a minimum of 1 (sources that state   directly, which no policy changes) and a maximum of 1.0204.

Two conclusions follow. First, the choice is a uniform scale of 2 % on the affected points: the weights of a weighted mean change by the same factor, so which points weigh more than others, and the weighted mean itself, do not change. Second, it matters only for the absolute size of the uncertainty of a derived quantity (the of the weighted mean, or the share of points inside the band of the previous section), and the difference of 2 % is small next to the scatter between laboratories.

Terms of the data you supply ​

The archive states one number per point, the expanded uncertainty of the property. A measurement of your own has more to say: the scatter of the repeated readings, the specification of each instrument, and the uncertainty of the temperature, the pressure and the composition at which the property was measured. ThermophysicalDB.jl keeps all of them, so that you can propagate them again through the model you fit (and so that error bars in and can be drawn).

Following the GUM (JCGM 100:2008) and NIST Technical Note 1297, each term is one of four kinds:

KindWhat it is
statisticaltype A: from the scatter of repeated observations,  
devicetype B: from the specification of the instrument, its calibration or its resolution
combinedthe terms combined,   for uncorrelated terms
expanded  , with its coverage factor

and every term is stored with its coverage: the value, the coverage factor (  for a standard uncertainty) and the level of confidence. A plain number is a standard uncertainty; a term stated at   or at 95 % is written UncertaintyTerm(0.02; k = 2) or UncertaintyTerm(0.02; level = 0.95). A term can belong to one point or to a whole experiment (the specification of a thermometer, shared by all its points), and it is about one quantity: the property, the temperature, the pressure or one of the compositions.

julia
using ThermophysicalDB

# the thermometer is common to the experiment 1 ...
set_uncertainty!(ds; scope = :experiment, row = 1, quantity = :temperature, device = 0.01)
# ... and each point adds what is its own
set_uncertainty!(ds; scope = :measurement, row = 17, quantity = :temperature,
                 statistical = 0.02)
set_uncertainty!(ds; scope = :measurement, row = 17, quantity = :property,
                 combined = 0.12)                      # expanded at k = 2 by default

error_T = expanded_uncertainty(view, :temperature)    # for the error bars, at k = 2
uncertainty_table(view)                                # every term, with its coverage

When the combined or the expanded uncertainty is missing it is derived, and the derived rows are marked as such and say how: a combined term you give is used as it is to compute the expanded one (the coverage of the call,   by default); an expanded term gives the combined one by  ; otherwise the combined uncertainty is the root sum of squares of the statistical and device terms of the point and of its experiment. For the property, the expanded uncertainty also fills the unc, unc_k and unc_level columns, so everything on this page works on your data like on the archive. The numbers you give are taken as they are, and so is their consistency: the package does not check a combined term against its parts.

Units and percentages ​

A number is in the unit that the database keeps: kelvin, kPa, the composition as stored and, for the property, the unit of its name. You do not have to convert what you have. A term may be a quantity of a units package, a text with a number and a unit, or a percentage, and it is converted for the point where it is stored:

julia
using ThermophysicalDB, Unitful

# the same 0.5 kg/m3 (the property is a density in kg/m3)
set_uncertainty!(ds; scope = :measurement, row = 17, quantity = :property,
                 statistical = 0.0005u"g/cm^3")
set_uncertainty!(ds; scope = :measurement, row = 17, quantity = :pressure,
                 statistical = "5 mbar")                # no package of units needed
set_uncertainty!(ds; scope = :measurement, row = 17, quantity = :temperature,
                 device = 0.01u"°C")                     # 0.01 K: a difference has no offset
set_uncertainty!(ds; scope = :measurement, row = 18, quantity = :property,
                 expanded = UncertaintyTerm("0.1 %"; k = 2))   # of the value at that point

An uncertainty is a difference between two values, so it is converted by the scale of the unit only: is  , not  . The conversion uses the exact arithmetic of the package (see Tables, units and Unitful); a unit whose symbol the package does not know is converted by your units package, and a quantity of the wrong dimension is an ArgumentError.

A percentage ("2 %", "500 ppm", 2u"percent") is that fraction of the value of the same quantity at the same point: the property in its unit, the temperature in kelvin, the pressure in kPa, the composition as stored. It is converted into the unit of the database as soon as it is stored, so the table always holds absolute uncertainties. The coverage is applied after the conversion: UncertaintyTerm("2 %"; k = 2) is a standard uncertainty of 1 % of the value. A percentage and a quantity of the property need a point (scope = :measurement), because an experiment may have several properties and its points have different values; at an experiment you can give the temperature or the pressure with a unit (the specification of a thermometer, for example) but not a percentage.

A percentage of a temperature is taken of the absolute scale ​

A relative uncertainty is a fraction of the value, and the value of a temperature depends on where the scale has its zero: 1 % of 25 °C is 0.25 K, but 1 % of 298.15 K is 2.98 K. The two are not the same measurement, and only the second means anything, because kelvin (and rankine) start at absolute zero. So the package always takes a percentage of the temperature in an absolute scale, whatever unit the temperature was written in:

Temperature given inThe percentage is taken ofResult
Kthe value in Kin K
°Cthe value in K (  )in K
°Fthe value in °R (  ), which is the same fraction of the value in Kin K (and in °R,   )

For example, "0.01 %" of 25 °C is 0.01 % of 298.15 K, that is 0.0298 K, and the table holds 0.0298 K. The same reasoning applies to the pressure, which is always taken of the absolute pressure in kPa (a gauge pressure is refused). An absolute uncertainty written with a unit is a difference and only its scale changes: "0.05 degC" is 0.05 K and "0.09 degF" is 0.05 K.

The uncertainties of the conditions of a point can be given where the point is added, without set_uncertainty!: the keywords unc_temperature, unc_pressure and unc_composition of add_measurement!, the columns unc_temperature, unc_pressure and unc_x1...unc_x3 of add_reference_data! and the u(T), u(P) and u(x) columns of the spreadsheet, all with the rules of this section. A plain number is a standard uncertainty ( ) and UncertaintyTerm(0.05; k = 2) states its coverage; the spreadsheet has the coverage factor of each column in its row k.

What is combined, and what is not

The combination above treats the terms of one quantity. The combined uncertainty of a property that you derive this way does not include the contribution of the uncertainty of , and composition, which depends on the model:   . For a liquid density near ambient conditions the term in usually dominates, since is large and small. The archive's combined uncertainty is supposed to include these contributions, but only 129,519 of its roughly 2.7 million uncertainties say that they were obtained by propagation. This is why the separate terms are kept: you can propagate them with your own model. In the ThermoML formats only the expanded uncertainty of the property is written, as the standard has it; the separate terms are in the .json of export_points and in uncertainty_table.
