Detecting outliers ​

find_outliers looks for measurements that are probably wrong. It works like points: you hand it a selection and it hands back selections, here three of them.

julia
using ThermophysicalDB

db = database()
v = points(db; component = "water", exact = true, property = "Mass density")

kept, outliers, disputed = find_outliers(v)      # three DataViews
result = find_outliers(v)                        # or keep the whole result
reasons(result)                                  # why each point was excluded
diagnostics(result)                              # the numbers behind the trend tests
ViewContains
keptpoints with no sign of being wrong
outlierspoints declared outliers, each with the reason
disputedpoints that one analysis questions and the others do not support: neither confirmed nor cleared

The three views partition the input and keep its order, so they can be passed to table, export_points or any other function that takes a selection.

A point that is not flagged is not validated

The tests compare a point with its neighbours. A point with too few neighbours is not tested, and the result says how many these are (result.counts.untested). A flag is a reason to look at a point, not a verdict: the physics can change regime where the data are (see Limitations), and the data of one publication are not independent of each other.

What is checked, in order ​

  1. Physical rules. Conditions that no real measurement can break. A point that fails is an outlier and takes no part in the next analyses.

  2. Trend along one axis. Points of the same system, property and phase that share the other conditions are put in order along , or composition. A window of neighbouring points is fitted with a robust parabola, and each point is compared with the curve fitted without it.

  3. Joint surface. When two or more axes vary (for instance and ), a quadratic surface with cross terms is fitted to the nearest points in all axes at once. It finds points that are consistent along each axis and inconsistent together, and, in a mixture, points that fit the mixture's own surface but not the neighbouring compositions.

The distances of the points to their curves are then pooled and tested with the generalized ESD test, which finds several outliers at once.

Stage 0: the physical rules ​

Only rules that follow from thermodynamic stability or from the definition of the quantity are used, so a point rejected here is wrong rather than merely unusual. Nothing is rejected for being large or small unless a ceiling is known: the ceilings are yours to give with known_limits.

julia
find_outliers(v; known_limits = Dict("Mass density" => (0, 2500)))   # either side may be nothing

Stage 1: a robust parabola in a small window ​

For every line of points, with the points ordered along the axis and a window of 9 neighbours, the model is

Why a parabola. A property is a smooth function of and ; over a few points a straight line misses the curvature and a higher degree follows the noise. The parabola also gives the first and second derivatives, which are reported as diagnostics.

Why robust. An ordinary fit is pulled towards the outlier it should reveal. The consensus of a window is found by exhaustive least quantile of squares ((Rousseeuw, 1984), (Rousseeuw and Leroy, 1987)): the parabola through every triple of points is tried and the one for which 60 % of the window fits best (consensus_fraction) wins. It is the same idea as RANSAC ((Fischler and Bolles, 1981)) but tries all the triples instead of random ones, so it is deterministic and, with 9 points, cheap. The consensus is then refitted by weighted least squares,  , and points are added or removed until the set is stable.

The statistic. The distance of point to the curve fitted without it, in units of the expected scatter, is its standardized deleted residual

with   ,  ,   and leverage   ((Cook, 1977), (Belsley et al., 1980)). A point with high leverage pulls the curve towards itself, which is why its residual is divided by , and a point left out of the fit pays the uncertainty of the extrapolation.

Uncertainties decide the tolerance. A point with a small is held to a tighter curve than one with a large , which is how accurate data weigh more. When a window has no declared uncertainty, the median of the known ones is used; when none is known,   and is estimated from the scatter alone. Sources are compared with each other. Points of different articles and methods share a line if they are the same property, system and phase under the same other conditions. This is the point of the analysis: a source that disagrees with several others is the outlier.

Which points share a line. Points belong to the same line when the other varying axes agree within tolerance: 0.1 K for , 1 % of the value for and for compositions. Lines shorter than min_points = 7, or windows with fewer than 4 distinct values, are not tested.

Stage 2: the joint surface ​

When two or more axes vary, each with at least 3 distinct values, the model is the full quadratic in the varying axes, with cross terms: with axes,     coefficients (6 for and , 10 for three axes). Its window is the nearest points (neighbors) in axes scaled by their range, and the same is computed. Windows whose design is nearly collinear (variance inflation factor above , (Belsley et al., 1980)) are discarded, because their coefficients would be rounding errors. The cross term is reported on the :joint rows of diagnostics.

This stage is the expensive one, so it is organised to spend effort only where it can change the result:

  1. Tiles. Points that sit close together (the same cell of a grid over the scaled coordinates, at most tile = 3 of them) share one window and one fit; each member gets its own deleted residual from it, exactly as the points of a line window do in stage 1. The neighbours come from the grid, not from a search through the whole subgroup, so the cost grows with the number of points, not with its square. tile = 1 fits every point on its own.

  2. Screening. Every tile is first fitted with a cheap robust procedure: least squares, discard what lies beyond inlier_threshold robust standard deviations (MAD of the residuals), and reweight until the set is stable. A tile with no point beyond screening * inlier_threshold (0.7 × 2.5) keeps this result. The tiles with a suspicious point, and the tiles of the closest neighbours of every point beyond inlier_threshold (a cluster of wrong values can hide from a cheap fit), go to step 3.

  3. Least quantile of squares. The consensus of those tiles is found with a least quantile of squares fit ((Rousseeuw, 1984), (Rousseeuw and Leroy, 1987)), as for the parabolas but over 20p subsets of points instead of all of them, refined by concentration steps (refit on the points closest to the current surface, which cannot make their fit worse). The subsets come from a small counter-based generator seeded by seed and the number of the tile, so the choice is deterministic: it does not change with the number of threads or between runs. It replaces the RANSAC with a ladder of scales that earlier versions used, which repeated a randomised search about seven times per point.

screening = 0 runs step 3 on every tile, the slowest and most conservative setting.

For a mixture, a pure substance is the end of the composition axis, so a point of a mixture is compared with its neighbours in , and a wrong value of the pure fluid shows up as a surface that bends towards it.

From tests to categories ​

A point is classified by the first rule that applies:

  1. A failed physical rule makes it an :outlier (code = :physical_rule).

  2. If at least 3 points of the same publication in a window deviate, 75 % or more of them on the same side and beyond the threshold, and other publications are present, they are a systematic offset of the source: :disputed with code = :source_offset (:outlier with source_offset = :outlier). An offset is a property of the whole series, not of a point.

  3. A point flagged by the ESD test along one axis is an :outlier (code = :trend_1d).

  4. A point flagged only by the joint surface is :disputed (code = :joint_surface): it is odd in the larger space, but its line has not confirmed it.

The trend reasons carry a hint when the deviation is a power of ten (a unit slip) or 273.15 (a Celsius–kelvin slip).

What the tables contain ​

reasons has one row per reason, with the point's position (index, measurement), category, code, axis, the ESD statistic, critical and rank, , the value the local fit predicts (reference_value) and the deviation from it, a detail in words, and the columns of table that identify the point. diagnostics has one row per point and analysis with , the window size, the number of points in agreement, , the slope and curvature with their standard errors, the influence of the point on the slope (a DFBETAS-like measure), the sign_test, the cross term, and whether ESD flagged it. The slope and curvature are diagnostics, not tests: a high curvature, a large or a high influence tell you the data are hard to judge there.

Limitations ​

  • The physics can change regime. A local parabola assumes the property is smooth. It is not at the triple point (the kink between sublimation and vaporisation of CO₂), in the critical region (thermal diffusivity, heat capacities, compressibility), or where a phase boundary is crossed within a line. Points on both sides of such a place can be flagged without being wrong. Look at the curvature and of the neighbours, and select one phase at a time.

  • Not flagged is not validated. See the counts of untested and single_source: a point checked only against its own publication has been checked for noise, not for bias.

  • Correlated data. Points of one publication are not independent of each other, which the statistics assume. That is why a systematic offset has its own category.

  • Scale of the scatter. With honest declared uncertainties the false-alarm rate is low (in simulations of clean data with 1 point in 1000 flagged). When the real scatter is larger than the declared uncertainty, or no uncertainty is declared, the scatter is estimated from windows of 9 points; that estimate is optimistic and more clean points are flagged (about 6 % with noise three times the declared uncertainty, about 8 % without uncertainties). Prefer data with declared uncertainties for the analysis, and treat flags in noisy data as candidates.

  • Rules between properties ( ,  , ...) are not applied; see above.

  • The statistics assume nothing about the model of the fluid. There is no equation of state here: the curve is local. Using an equation of state to judge all properties at once is a later step.
